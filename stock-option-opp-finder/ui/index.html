In plain English

Selling premium means: someone pays you money today because they want protection. You keep the money if the stock stays calm. You can lose much more than you collected if the stock jumps — so we only look at trades with a protective second option that caps the worst loss. That pair is called a credit spread.

Our plan in the tests: collect the money, then close the trade after 1–2 days (or sooner if half the money is already safe). Never hold and hope until the last day.

The honest headline: in one year of tests, nothing proved itself. The friendliest pattern was selling right after a stock got stretched (a big quick drop or pop), because stretched moves often calm down. It happened too few times to trust yet.

Why are there so few real option trades in the tests?

Our free historical option data only shows a sample of strikes each day, and the sample changes daily. Usually the exact options we "sold" had no price the next day, so we refused to guess. Green shows how often we could honestly check the result.

Words we use

Premium
The money the buyer pays you. It is yours to keep only if you close the trade safely.
Credit spread
Sell one option, buy a cheaper one further away as protection. Your worst loss is known on day one.
Strike
The price level in the option bet. Sell a strike away from today's price so the stock has room to move.
Delta (0.30)
Roughly a 30% chance the bet ends in the money. We sold near 0.30 in the tests — not too close, not too far.
IV (implied volatility)
How much movement the option price is charging for. High IV means expensive options — good for sellers, if the stock then behaves.
Breach
The stock moved more than expected. That is when premium sellers get hurt.
Expectancy
Average result per trade. It must be positive over many trades before a pattern means anything.
